# A short history of kinetic typography

Type has wanted to move for a long time. Long before film, printers and poets played with type that implies
movement and time on the page. Cinema made it literal, television made it everyday, and desktop software and
social video made it one of the most common forms of motion design.

## The arc

- **On the page**: experimental printing and poetry arranged type to suggest sound, speed and sequence,
  turning reading into a timed experience.
- **Silent-film title cards**: text on screen as narration and dialogue, sometimes with simple animation.
- **Designed film titles (1950s–60s)**: credits move with the music and the shapes, become part of the
  composition, and in some films sweep along architectural lines ([mid-century title design](mid-century-title-design.md)).
- **Television (1960s–90s)**: animated type in commercials, station idents and news graphics; rapid-cut,
  hand-lettered and later computer-generated type.
- **Desktop and web (1990s–2000s)**: affordable software made animated type a staple; typed-out dialogue set
  to film audio became a popular exercise and a genre.
- **Lyric videos and social (2010s–now)**: official lyric videos, word-by-word captions and text-only ads;
  kinetic type as the main visual for sound-off viewing.

## A vocabulary of moving type

Fragmentation (letters split apart), repetition (a word echoed), rhythm (words on beats), variation (size and
weight change across a line), distortion (stretch, squash), transparency and layering, masking (words from
behind edges), and abstraction (letters becoming shapes). Most kinetic pieces combine two or three.

## Why it matters for motion

- Text is often the cheapest, clearest image available; making it move well is a core skill.
- The vocabulary above is a menu for choosing a treatment that matches meaning.
