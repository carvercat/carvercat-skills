# Mid-century film title design

In the 1950s and 1960s, film titles changed from static credit cards into designed short films. Graphic
designers brought modernist posters into cinema: a single symbol, flat cut-paper shapes, bold colour and type
that moved with the music. Much of what motion designers still call "titles" was invented here.

## What defined it

- **One symbol for the whole film**: a jagged arm, a spiral, fragmented lines; the title sequence distilled
  the story into one graphic idea that also became the poster.
- **Cut-paper flatness**: simple shapes, hard edges, a limited palette (often black, white and one strong
  colour), no illusion of depth.
- **Type in motion**: credits sliding along lines, splitting, aligning with architectural grids; type became a
  moving part of the composition.
- **Music as structure**: jazz and orchestral scores drove the cutting and the rhythm of shapes.
- Other strands: rapid-cut, hand-lettered titles over live action; elaborate opening sequences for spy and
  epic films; early computer-generated spirals produced with converted analog machinery.

## Why it matters for motion

- It proved that motion graphics can carry tone and theme on their own.
- The vocabulary (flat shapes, bold type, rhythmic cutting, a single concept) maps directly onto 2D tools.
- It shows restraint: a few elements, perfectly timed, outlast elaborate effects.

## Using the influence well

- Find the one symbol; build the sequence from it.
- Flat colour, a limited palette, slight imperfection (cut-paper edges, stepped motion on twos) for warmth.
- Let type align to the shapes' lines and edges.
- Cut and move on the music's accents.
