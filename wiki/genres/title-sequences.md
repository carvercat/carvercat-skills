# Title sequences

A title sequence opens a film or series: it sets the mood, introduces the world and carries the credits.
The best ones are short films in their own right, built on one strong idea that tells you how to feel
before the story starts.

## The idea

- **One concept**: an object, a texture, a metaphor or a graphic system that stands for the story (a
  character's notebooks, a spiral, a map, a single repeated shape).
- **Type is the protagonist**: the credits are not overlays; they live in the world (on surfaces, in masks,
  cut by edges, revealed by light).
- **Rhythm with the music**: the main theme drives the cut. Hard sync for tension, loose drift for mood.
- **Credit order and legibility** are contractual: each name must be readable for long enough, in the agreed
  order and size.

## Rules of thumb

- Feature film openings 1.5–3 min; series openers 30–90 s (streaming shows often 30–60 s and skippable).
- Each credit card readable for its full read time; for one or two names about 2–3 s.
- One or two typefaces; the treatment carries the variety.
- Repeat a visual motif three times (introduce, develop, resolve on the main title).
- Land the main title on the strongest musical moment.

## Do and don't

- Do find the single idea before designing frames; test it as a still.
- Do treat credits as part of the composition (masked behind objects, aligned to lines in the image).
- Don't let effects compete with names; when a name is on, the image calms down.
- Don't reuse a famous sequence's signature move without reason; quote the mood, not the scene.

## Checklist

- [ ] One concept, stated in one sentence.
- [ ] Every credit readable (reading time) in the required order.
- [ ] Main title lands on the musical peak.
- [ ] The sequence also works muted (some platforms autoplay silently).
